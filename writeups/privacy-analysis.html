Finding People in "Anonymous" Data

Deleting the name column does not make data anonymous. Here is the attack that proves it, and the methods that actually do protect people.

Tools
pandas · NumPy · matplotlib
Attacks
Linkage, differencing
Defences
Added noise, randomised answers
Models
Central and local

Part 1 — The attack

The data set had names and ID numbers, plus details like age, ZIP code, education, and salary. The normal advice is to drop the obvious identifiers and publish the rest: 

adult_data = adult.copy().drop(
    columns=['Name', 'First Name', 'Last Name', 'SSN'])

What is left looks harmless. But those remaining columns are what we call quasi-identifiers. On their own they identify nobody. Together they often point to exactly one person. Date of birth plus ZIP code is the classic example.

So an attacker who knows a few facts about their target, from a public record or just from knowing them personally, can match that against the published data: 

target_row = adult_pii[adult_pii['Name'] == TARGET_NAME]

pd.merge(target_row, adult_data,
         left_on=['DOB', 'Zip'], right_on=['DOB', 'Zip'])

If that match returns exactly one row, you have found your person. Every private detail in the published data, including their salary, is now attached to a real name. This is the same method that has been used before to pull individuals out of released medical records, and out of a supposedly anonymous set of film ratings by comparing it to public reviews.

Publishing only totals does not fix it

The next idea is to publish averages and totals instead of individual rows. That fails too. Group the data finely enough and some groups contain only one person. Then the "average" is that one person's exact value: 

adult[['Zip', 'Age']].groupby('Zip', as_index=False).mean()

It gets worse. Even totals over large groups leak, through what is called a differencing attack. Ask for the total salary of all staff. Then ask for the total salary of all staff except one named person. Neither question looks sensitive on its own. Subtract one from the other and you have that person's exact salary.

Part 2 — What actually works

Differential privacy fixes this at the root. The idea is to add a carefully measured amount of random noise to every answer, so that the answer looks almost the same whether or not any one person is in the data at all.

Now the differencing attack fails. Both totals are noisy, so the difference is noisy too, and that noise is bigger than the one salary the attacker was trying to isolate. The important part is that this holds no matter what other information the attacker already has. Simply removing names can never promise that.

Removing the need for trust

There is still a catch. This approach needs someone trustworthy to hold everyone's real data and add the noise. Often that is exactly the company you are worried about, such as a platform collecting data from its own users.

The local model removes them from the picture. Each person adds noise to their own answer before it ever leaves their device. I implemented randomised response, the original method for this, invented for surveys back in 1965.

It works like this. You are asked an embarrassing yes-or-no question. You flip a coin in private. Depending on the result, you either answer honestly or you give a fixed answer. Now no single answer proves anything about you, because you can always say the coin decided it. But the researcher still learns the truth about the group, because they know exactly how the coin affects the totals and can cancel it out across thousands of people.

The trade-off, measured

The local model gives stronger privacy but less accurate results. Noise gets added once per person instead of once per question, so the errors pile up, and only some kinds of questions can be answered at all. Measuring that gap, and how much accuracy you lose for a given level of privacy, was the main analysis work here.

What this shows

  • Real data analysis with pandas and NumPy, aimed at an attacker's question rather than a business report.
  • Understanding why deleting columns fails once an attacker has outside information, and why differential privacy is a different kind of promise.
  • Building privacy methods in both models and measuring what they cost, instead of just claiming they work.
  • The habit of attacking a defence before trusting it.
