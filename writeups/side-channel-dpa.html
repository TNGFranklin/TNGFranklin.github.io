Side-Channel Security

Finding AES Keys by Watching Power

The maths was correct and the code was correct. The key still came out. A chip uses slightly different amounts of power depending on the data it is handling, and you can measure that.

Target
AES-128 on an STM32F chip
Equipment
ChipWhisperer Nano
Measurements
500 encryptions
Samples each
11,000

The idea

Inside a chip, flipping a bit from 0 to 1 costs a little more power than leaving it alone. Add that up across the whole chip and the power it draws at each moment depends on the data it is working on. If some of that data depends on the secret key, then the power is leaking the key.

This is not something I only read about. I measured it myself. I ran AES-128 on a real STM32F chip, recorded its power with a ChipWhisperer Nano, and captured 500 encryptions of random data at 11,000 samples each. 

pts          = np.random.randint(256, size=(500, 16), dtype=np.uint8)
traces, cts  = measure(scope, target, pts)   # 500 x 11000 numbers

Breaking the key into small pieces

Trying every AES key is impossible. There are 2128 of them. The attack works because AES lets you go after one byte of the key at a time.

Early in the process, AES computes SBOX(data[i] ⊕ key[i]). That value depends on just one byte of data, which I know, and one byte of the key, which I do not. So there are only 256 options for that byte. Sixteen bytes at 256 options each is 4,096 guesses in total, instead of 2128.

For each guess I predict how much power the chip should use, then split my 500 measurements into groups based on that prediction. The correct guess splits them in a way that matches what the chip really did, so the two groups have clearly different average power at one moment in time. Wrong guesses split them randomly, so their groups look the same. The correct byte shows up as a sharp spike.

Measuring how well the attack works

Saying "it worked" is not very useful. The better question is how many measurements you need. So I tested the attack every 25 traces and tracked two numbers against the real key:

  • Success rate — how many of the 16 key bytes the attack got right.
  • Average rank — where the real byte sits in the sorted list of guesses. This is more useful early on, because it improves smoothly even while the success rate is still zero.
increments = np.arange(25, traces.shape[0] + 1, 25)
srs        = np.zeros_like(increments, dtype=float)
avg_ranks  = np.zeros_like(increments, dtype=float)

The graphs show the usual pattern. The rank drops towards first place long before the success rate climbs, because the right answer is usually near the top of the list well before it wins outright.

Beating the defence

In the second part, the chip ran a protected version of AES. It used a trick called hiding in time: it inserts a random number of do-nothing instructions before the important step, so the leak no longer happens at the same moment in every measurement.

This breaks my attack at its foundation. All my maths assumes that sample number 500 means the same thing in every trace. Now it does not. Averaging traces that are out of step smears the signal away into the noise.

But the defence does not remove the leak. It only moves it around. So I lined the traces up first: I found a recognisable pattern in each one and shifted the trace until they all matched. Once they were back in step, the same attack worked again, and the key came out.

The lesson

A defence that only adds randomness makes an attack more expensive, but it does not stop it. If the leak is still there, you can clean up the data and go again. The defences that hold are the ones that break the link between the secret and what you can measure, such as masking.

Related work

Alongside this I studied PUFs, or physically unclonable functions. These are circuits that give different answers on every chip because of tiny random differences from manufacturing. The idea is to use them as keys that cannot be copied, since even the maker cannot reproduce one.

Using pypuf I simulated 64-bit arbiter and XOR-arbiter PUFs, generated 10,000 challenge and response pairs, and looked at how well the simple designs hold up. The attack here is modelling: if you collect enough pairs, you can train a model that predicts the PUF's answers, and you have copied the thing that was supposed to be uncopiable.

What this shows

  • Security depends on real hardware, not just on the algorithm on paper.
  • I am comfortable with lab equipment, noisy measurements, and statistics, not only with simulations.
  • Reporting how much effort an attack needs is more useful than reporting that it succeeded.
  • Understanding how a defence works is what tells you whether you can undo it.
Note

Done on lab hardware with firmware supplied by the course, as part of a university module on side-channel security.
