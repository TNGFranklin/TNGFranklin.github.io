Privacy Theatre in Crypto Wallets

Crypto wallets are sold on the promise that you hold your own keys and nobody is watching. There is only one way to check that promise: watch what the app actually sends.

Subject
A self-custody wallet app
Platform
Rooted Android emulator
Tools
Burp Suite · adb
Status
Write-up in progress

The question

"Non-custodial" means one specific thing: the company cannot move your money. That is all it means. But many users hear something much bigger, and assume the company learns nothing about them. Those are two different promises, and the gap between them is where privacy theatre lives.

Holding your own keys says nothing about whether your wallet address, your balance, your phone's ID, and your daily habits are being sent to other companies.

This matters more than usual with a blockchain. Everything on it is public but under a random address, so you are hidden only as long as nobody links that address to your name. If an app sends your address together with a permanent phone ID, the app has made that link for you. It happens off the blockchain, it lasts forever, and you cannot see it.

Method

Most of the work is just getting into a position to see the traffic at all. The setup was:

  • A rooted emulator, which gives the deep access needed to make any of this possible.
  • A proxy with its certificate installed at system level, so encrypted traffic can be read. On modern Android, a normally installed certificate is not trusted by apps. It has to go into the system store.
  • Reading the app package first, to list what permissions it asks for and which other companies' code is bundled inside it. This tells you what to look for before you start.
  • Using the app normally — install it, create a wallet, check a balance, move around — while recording every request it makes.

The two halves work together. The package tells you what the app can do. The recorded traffic tells you what it does do. Any gap between those two, in either direction, is worth looking at.

What I look for

What to checkWhy it matters
Which other companies it contactsSeparates servers the app truly needs from tracking and marketing services
Wallet addresses inside requestsLinks your public blockchain address to a private profile
Permanent device or install IDsLets someone follow you across sessions and reinstalls

Being careful

Seeing an app contact a tracking company's server is not the same as proving what it sent there. Any claim has to be based on reading the actual contents of the request. "A tracking library is included" and "this exact piece of data left the phone" are very different statements, and mixing them up is the most common mistake in this kind of research.

Status

The test setup, the package analysis, and the traffic recordings are all done. The written study is on its third draft. I am finishing the results before publishing them here.

What this shows

  • Real mobile security testing: preparing a rooted device, reading encrypted traffic from an app that resists it, and combining package analysis with live testing.
  • Reading a privacy promise closely enough to see what it does not cover.
  • Turning a question into a research plan and building the setup needed to answer it.
  • Keeping what I observed separate from what I concluded.
Note

Done in an isolated lab setup using a test wallet with no real money in it, as university research.
