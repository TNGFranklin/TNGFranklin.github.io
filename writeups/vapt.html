Vulnerability Assessment and Penetration Testing

This is the work I was paid to do. Clients handed me their networks, firewalls, databases, routers and data centres, and asked a simple question: if someone came for us, what would they get?

Role
Information Security Analyst, Red Team
Perspectives
External and internal
Scope
Networks · firewalls · databases · routers · data centres
Frameworks
NIST CSF · ISO 27001 · ITGC

The two halves, and why both are needed

VAPT is two different jobs joined together, and clients often do not realise they are buying both.

  • Vulnerability assessment is about breadth. Cover everything, find every weakness you can, and rank them. It answers "how much is wrong, and what should we fix first?"
  • Penetration testing is about depth. Take the weaknesses and prove what they actually allow. It answers "is this real, and how far does it go?"

An assessment without testing produces a long list where nobody knows which items matter. Testing without an assessment finds one impressive path and misses the other forty issues sitting beside it. Run together, the assessment tells you where to look and the test tells you which findings deserve a client's budget.

Outside in, then inside out

I tested from both perspectives, because they answer different questions.

External testing takes the position of an attacker on the internet with no access and no knowledge. What does the organisation expose? Which services are reachable that nobody realised were reachable? Forgotten hosts, management interfaces open to the world, and services still running after the project that needed them ended are common findings.

Internal testing assumes that has already failed — a phishing email worked, or a laptop was stolen, or a contractor is on the network. From that position, how far can someone move? This is where organisations with a hard perimeter tend to do badly, because everything inside was built assuming everything inside is trusted.

Why the internal test matters more than clients expect

Every organisation eventually has someone unauthorised inside the network. The question that decides the damage is not whether that happens, but what one foothold is worth once it does.

Validation is the job

Anyone can run a scanner. The output is a few hundred findings, a large share of which are wrong — a version banner that has been patched without changing the banner, a service flagged as vulnerable that is not reachable from anywhere that matters, a check that fired on the wrong thing.

Handing that raw list to a client is worse than useless. They spend weeks on findings that were never real, lose faith in the report, and stop reading the parts that mattered. Every finding I reported was one I had confirmed by hand.

The reverse is just as important. The most serious findings are usually the ones no scanner reports: a database reachable from a network segment it should never have been reachable from, a firewall rule that made sense years ago, credentials shared between a test system and a production one. Those come from understanding how the environment fits together, not from a tool.

Across the infrastructure

  • Networks — segmentation that does not hold, hosts that can reach things they should not, and traffic that should have been encrypted and is not.
  • Firewalls — reviewing rulesets rather than only testing through them. Rules accumulate for years, and old permissive rules survive long after the reason for them is gone.
  • Databases — authentication, privileges, exposure to the wider network, and whether sensitive data is protected at rest.
  • Routers — default and weak credentials, exposed management interfaces, outdated firmware, and insecure protocols still enabled.
  • Data centres — the infrastructure everything else depends on, where a single weakness affects every system hosted on it.
  • Mobile applications — static and dynamic analysis, covered separately in its own writeup.

Testing and auditing answer different questions

Alongside the technical work I ran IT security audits against NIST CSF, ISO 27001, and ITGC controls.

The distinction is worth being precise about. Testing tells you what is broken today. Auditing tells you whether the process that let it break will let it break again next quarter. A client who only tests keeps paying to rediscover the same class of problem. A client who only audits has documented processes and no evidence anyone follows them.

The report is the deliverable

The client does not buy the testing. They buy the report, and a finding nobody acts on may as well not have been found. What makes one usable:

  • Impact in business terms. Not "SMB signing is disabled" but what an attacker gets because of it, and which systems that puts at risk.
  • Ratings that survive scrutiny. Severity has to account for reachability and real-world exploitability, not just a generic score. Everything marked critical means nothing is.
  • Evidence for every claim. Enough for the client's team to reproduce it. If they cannot reproduce it, they cannot confirm they have fixed it.
  • Remediation that fits the client. Specific, and achievable with the staff and budget they actually have.
  • Two audiences. A summary that a director can act on, and technical detail an engineer can work from. Same report, different sections.

What this shows

  • Full engagements against real client infrastructure, under contract, from scoping to retest.
  • Testing across the whole estate — network, perimeter, database, and application — rather than one specialism.
  • Validating every finding by hand, which is what separates an assessment from a scanner report.
  • Reading configuration and architecture to find the issues no tool reports.
  • Working fluently in both offensive testing and control-framework auditing.
  • Writing for the people who have to act on it.
