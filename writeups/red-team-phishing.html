Red Teaming and Phishing Simulations

You can patch every server in a company and still lose it to one convincing email. My job was to send that email first, with permission, and turn what happened into something the organisation could act on.

Permission comes first

The only thing separating this work from a crime is authorisation, so that is where every engagement starts. Before a single email goes out there has to be a signed agreement covering what may be tested, which staff are in scope, the dates the campaign runs, and who at the client is told in advance.

That last point matters more than people expect. Someone senior has to approve it, and usually one or two people in IT need to know, so that a report from an alert employee does not turn into a real incident response at three in the morning. Getting this right is part of the professional skill, not paperwork around it.

Building the campaign

A phishing simulation needs to behave like the real thing, or the results mean nothing. I built the infrastructure for these campaigns using:

  • GoPhish as the campaign platform — it manages the recipient lists, sends the emails, hosts the landing pages, and records what each person does.
  • Mailgun for delivery, so the mail comes from properly configured infrastructure and actually arrives instead of vanishing into spam folders.
  • A registered domain behind Cloudflare, with Nginx in front of the landing pages, so the simulation is served over HTTPS like any normal site.

If the email lands in spam or the page throws a certificate warning, you have not measured anything about your staff. You have measured your own setup.

Designing the pretext

The message itself is the part that decides whether a test is fair. A badly written email with obvious spelling mistakes teaches you nothing, because it only catches people who were not reading. A pretext that abuses genuine trust — a fake message from a named colleague about a real personal matter — catches everyone but leaves staff feeling tricked by their own employer, and that damages the security culture you were hired to improve.

The useful ground is in between: messages modelled on the routine business the organisation actually runs, of the kind a real attacker would send after a little research. Running these at a few levels of difficulty is more informative than one campaign, because the point is not a pass or fail. It is finding where the line sits.

The metric people get wrong

Clients want the click rate driven to zero. It never gets there, and chasing it is the wrong goal. Someone will always click, so the question that decides the outcome is what happens in the next ten minutes. An organisation where 20% click but half of them report it within minutes is in far better shape than one where 5% click and nobody says anything, because the first one gets a chance to respond and the second one does not know it has been breached.

Reporting it well

The deliverable is a report, and how it is written decides whether anything improves. Two rules I worked to:

  • No naming individuals. Results go to management as figures and patterns, never as a list of who failed. The moment staff believe the exercise is about catching them personally, they stop reporting anything, and reporting is the one behaviour worth building.
  • Findings have to be actionable. "Staff need more awareness" is not a finding. Which departments were most exposed, which pretexts worked, whether the mail filters caught anything, how long until the first report, and what specifically to change — those are findings.

Captured credentials are treated as the sensitive data they are: recorded as an event, never retained or used, and destroyed with the rest of the engagement data afterwards.

The rest of the red team work

Phishing was one part of a broader testing role. Across client engagements I also carried out vulnerability assessments and penetration tests against networks, firewalls, databases, routers, and data centre infrastructure — from outside the network as an unknown attacker, and from inside as someone who had already got a foothold.

Alongside the technical testing I ran IT security audits against the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, ISO 27001, and ITGC controls. Testing tells you what is broken today; auditing tells you whether the process that broke it will break it again next quarter. Clients generally need both answers, and the combination is what makes a report worth paying for.

What this shows

  • I can stand up believable campaign infrastructure — mail delivery, domains, HTTPS, and the platform tying it together — not just click through a tool.
  • I treat authorisation, scope, and data handling as part of the work, because with social engineering they are the whole difference between testing and attacking.
  • I understand which numbers predict whether an organisation survives a real phishing attack, and it is not the one clients ask for.
  • I write results in a way that improves behaviour rather than punishing staff.
  • Comfort across the full range of an engagement: people, network, applications, and control frameworks.
