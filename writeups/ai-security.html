AI Security

Attacking Machine Learning Models

A model that scores 98.94% on its test set can still be fully under an attacker's control. I built the attacks that do it, and measured exactly how much damage each one causes.

Framework
PyTorch
Attacks built
Poisoning, backdoors, evasion, sponge
Best result
100% backdoor success
Defences
Robust training, trojan detection

Why AI security is different

In normal software, the rules are written by a person, so you can read them. In machine learning, the rules are learned from data. That moves the attack surface. You no longer have to break the code. You can attack the data it learns from, the model file itself, or the inputs it sees when it is running.

Worse, the usual test does not catch any of this. Accuracy on a clean test set is the standard way to check a model, and every attack below leaves that number looking healthy.

Poisoning the training data

The first attack is the simplest. If I can change some of the training data, I can change what the model learns. I flipped the labels on a portion of the training set, so the model was taught wrong answers, then measured what that cost.

Look at how small those drops are. Poisoning a fifth of the entire training set costs less than one percentage point of accuracy. Nobody watching a dashboard would notice. This is the useful finding: broad random poisoning is loud in effort but quiet in effect, so it is a poor attack.

Targeted poisoning is the better version. Instead of flipping labels at random, I flipped every example of one class into another — 3,065 images of a "3" relabelled as "8". That concentrates all the damage into the one decision the attacker cares about, and leaves overall accuracy nearly untouched. I measured it with attack success rate: the share of that one class the model now gets wrong in the attacker's chosen direction.

Backdoors: the dangerous one

A backdoor is the attack that should worry people. Rather than making the model worse, you teach it one extra secret rule: when a specific trigger pattern is present in the input, ignore everything else and output the attacker's chosen answer.

The results from my implementation:

MeasurementResult
Accuracy on normal inputs98.94%
Backdoor success rate100.0%

Both numbers at once is the whole point. The model behaves perfectly on every normal input, so it passes every test anyone would think to run. But whenever the trigger appears, it obeys the attacker — every single time, with no failures. The backdoor costs nothing in visible quality.

Why this is a supply chain problem

Most teams do not train models from scratch. They download a pretrained model and adapt it. If the backdoor was planted before you downloaded it, you inherit it, and no accuracy test you run will reveal it. This is the same risk as installing a compromised software package, except the malicious behaviour is spread across millions of numbers instead of sitting in readable code.

Sponge attacks: denial of service

The third attack does not care about the model's answers at all. It attacks the bill. AI models do not use a fixed amount of work per request, so an attacker can craft inputs that are deliberately expensive to process. I modelled this against a prediction service:

MeasurementNormalUnder attack
Response time0.32 ms0.92 ms (+184%)
Requests served3,090/sec1,088/sec (−65%)
Time to overload—2.6 seconds
Revenue$1,669/hour$588/hour

That last row is what makes this land with a business. The service does not go down, so no alarm fires for an outage. It just gets three times slower, breaks its response-time guarantees, and quietly loses about $1,081 an hour. I also tested a lower-powered variant, which raised response times 26% without breaking the guarantee at all — slow enough to hurt, quiet enough to go unreported.

Evasion at run time

Evasion attacks change the input rather than the model. You take something the model classifies correctly and add a small, carefully chosen amount of noise, calculated from the model's own sensitivity, so the answer flips. To a person the input looks unchanged. This is the attack that matters for anything doing filtering, fraud scoring, or recognition, because the attacker controls the input by definition.

Building the defences too

Attacking is only half of it. I also implemented and tested the countermeasures:

  • Robust training — train the model on attacked examples on purpose, so it learns to hold up under them. It works, and it costs accuracy on normal inputs. That trade-off is the real decision.
  • Trojan detection — examine a model you did not train and look for signs of a hidden trigger, without knowing what the trigger is.
  • Certified defences — instead of testing a model against known attacks and hoping, prove mathematically that no input within a certain distance can change the answer. A guarantee rather than a hope.

Wider coverage

Alongside the hands-on labs, the study covered the rest of the field: attacks that steal a model or recover its private training data, defences for generative and agentic AI systems, explainable AI as a security tool, security of federated learning where training is spread across many devices, synthetic data generation, and the legal and forensic side of AI incidents.

What this shows

  • I can build these attacks in PyTorch, not just describe them.
  • I measure impact in numbers a decision-maker can use — attack success rate, accuracy cost, requests per second, money per hour.
  • I understand why standard accuracy testing misses every attack here, which is the gap most teams have.
  • I have implemented the defences as well, including their costs, so I can advise on the trade-off rather than just listing options.
Note

Carried out as hands-on lab work in a university course on data and AI security, using standard image datasets and models trained locally. No production or third-party systems were involved.
